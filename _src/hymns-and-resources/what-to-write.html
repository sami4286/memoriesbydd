What to Write

There is no single correct way to write an order of service, but there is a shape most follow. This is that shape, with wording you are welcome to copy and change.

What do you write in a funeral order of service?

A funeral order of service usually carries the front cover with their name and dates, the running order of the service, the words to any hymns, the readings, a short life story, and acknowledgements thanking those who helped. Most are four or eight pages.

The front cover

A photograph, their name, and two dates. Some families use the full name and some use the name everybody actually called them — both are right, and the one people will recognise is usually the better choice.

For example

In Loving Memory of
Clover Dana Whittick
Sunrise: 10th November 1972
Sunset: 9th September 2018

Sunrise and Sunset are common in Caribbean and African services. Born and Died, or simply the two years, are just as usual elsewhere.

The running order, line by line

This is the part guests actually follow, so it should be readable at arm’s length in a cold church. Name whoever is doing each part — people like to know who is speaking.

Entrance
Music as the family comes in
Opening words
The minister or celebrant
First hymn
Words printed in full
Reading
Read by a named family member
Tribute
One or two people, kept short
Second hymn
Words printed in full
Reflection
Music, or a moment of silence
Committal
At the graveside or the crematorium
Final hymn
Words printed in full
Closing words
And where everyone is invited afterwards

Writing a life story

Two hundred words is plenty. Where they were born, who they married, what work they did, what they were like to be around. One specific detail is worth more than a paragraph of praise — the way they answered the phone, what they grew, who they supported.

For example

Clover was born in Kingston in 1972, the third of five, and came to London at nineteen with one suitcase and a letter from her aunt.

She nursed at Northwick Park for thirty-one years. She never once left the house without her earrings in, and she could get a laugh out of anybody, including on the ward at four in the morning.

Writing a tribute

A tribute is spoken, so write it the way you talk. Three minutes is long enough. Pick one story that shows who they were, tell it properly, and stop — the room will do the rest.

If you are not sure you can get through it, ask someone to stand beside you and take over if you cannot. That is a normal thing to arrange, and it makes it much easier to start.

Acknowledgements

Usually the back page. Thank the people who helped — the hospital, the neighbours, whoever cooked — and say where everyone is welcome afterwards.

For example

The family of Clover Whittick would like to thank everyone for your prayers, cards, flowers and kindness at this time.

You are warmly invited to join us afterwards at the church hall for refreshments.

The back cover

A short poem, a favourite line of scripture, or simply a second photograph. It is the page people look at longest afterwards, so it is worth leaving quiet.
